Science: why 0.2778, and what a leaderboard-calibrated model says

1. The metric algebra that explains B2

The official metric (problem page) is DTI = TPw/(TPw+0.2·FPw+0.8·FNw), with a 300 m triangular kernel. Because FNw = |G| − TPw, this is DTI = TPw/(0.2·(TPw+FPw) + 0.8·|G|). For a fixed truth set G, adding a dot raises the score only if its marginal credit ΔT satisfies ΔT/(ΔT+ΔFP) > 0.2·DTI, which works out to about a 2% chance that the dot lands on a hidden fault. A dot exactly on a fault earns at most 3 truth pixels of credit.

The ground truth is expert-mapped faults that are not in the public catalogue. Dots near catalogue faults therefore earn almost nothing and pay the full FP price. GEMSDOE32's B2 is the GEMSDOE31 40,199-dot d2.8 dotted-ridge base minus the 2,545 dots within 3 px of the catalogue. Its score rose from 0.2708 to 0.2778 when only those dots were removed. That is the signature of dots with near-zero marginal credit. The lineage h19-5 0.1922 → d2.8 0.2600 → solo 0.2708 → B2 0.2778 is a sequence of FP-shedding moves on one fixed network, not a new signal. All nine focus files belong to it, and it saturates at about 0.28.

2. LBCAL: invert the leaderboard instead of guessing the target

Each archived raster with an owner-reported score is a soft measurement of where the hidden faults are. LBCAL models a hidden-fault density π over joint bins of seven layers: catalogue distance, det-elev curvature, slope crest, det-elev slope, 3DEP-LiDAR scarp rank, TMI horizontal gradient and SGMC distance. The expected DTI of any raster then follows exactly: T = Σπ·c (c is the raster's max-credit map), FP ≈ N − 3Σπ·p, and G = Σπ. The model has 34 coefficients, fitted by least squares to 119 unique scored arrays.

The selected model is pi_max=0.2|lam=0.0001. Its density is depleted within 300 m of the catalogue (1–1.4 px -3.55, ≥55 px +1.90), enriched on moderately steep slopes (70–95th percentile +5.75 vs flat -4.82) with low curvature (+3.21), and depleted on slope crests (-5.06) and on strong LiDAR scarps (top 3% -2.26 vs weak +3.91). In plain words, the public hidden faults sit on planar range-flank slopes about 1–5 km from mapped faults, not on the crisp scarps that the catalogue and the scarp detectors already find. Named mimic: landslide head-scarps, terrace risers and road cuts share the slope signature.

3. Design, and why it is still not cleared

A Dinkelbach batch-greedy max-coverage placement under π gives 71,416 dots. In-model it scores 0.573. The cross-fit (design on half of the families, evaluate under the other half; 4 runs) gives 0.501, with a family bootstrap of 0.493–0.604. These are ESTIMATES. The post-hoc neighbour check shows that the model over-predicts the dense emission rasters whose dots the design overlaps (owner/CV down to 0.66) and under-predicts the d2.8 lineage. The honest range is therefore 0.33–0.73, a heuristic only.

It fails the owner's holdout gate: HOLDOUT-DTI 0.0631 (95% CI 0.0513–0.0765) against 0.1292. It also fails the like-for-like uniqueness rule, because 81–91% of its dots fall inside the halos of four dense registry rasters (h23-a, h23-b, h30, ovtr) whose halos cover only 41–54% of the map.

4. The gate itself: experiment 3

All 119 unique scored rasters were run through the same hide-and-recover evaluator. HOLDOUT-DTI correlates with the OWNER-REPORTED public score at ρ = 0.089 (top-20: 0.023). LBCAL's family-CV estimate correlates at ρ = 0.914. The best holdout file (repo-c0-habitat-emission-20261003-a4d439, holdout 0.326) scored 0.0041 publicly. The champion B2 has holdout 0.0045. The catalogue hide-and-recover task rewards catalogue proximity, which is exactly what the leaderboard punishes. (Caveat: registry masks are static, not fold-local, so catalogue-hugging files get leaked credit.) Under the owner's rule the verdict is still NEGATIVE. Changing the rule is the owner's call.
